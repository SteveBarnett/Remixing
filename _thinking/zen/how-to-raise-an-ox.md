Notes from How To Raise An Ox by Francis Dojun Cook.

## Raw notes

### Preface

In the world of Zen training, where there is neither good nor bad, reading and study are no better or worse than any other activity.

### One – Introduction

- The Zen of Dogen is the Zen of practice.
- ... this making real does not happen in the absence of constant effort, which is the daily, regular, conscious direction of the will to that realisation.
- "wonderful practice based on intrinsic enlightenment"
- To believe that one does zazen now in order to acquire enlightenment later is merely to perpetuate and strengthen the very dualisms that lie at the root of the human problem.
- **"The oneness of practice and enlightenment"**
- This doctrine of intrinsic enlightenment is, in Dogen's teaching, raised up to the status of central and crucial fact.
- ... one sits quietly, without expectation, ... simply to enjoy the nature which is inherently one's own.
- **This Dharma is amply present in every person, but unless one practices, it is not manifested, unless there is realisation, it is not attained.**
- The whole point of Buddhism as an experiential religion is this real-making process whereby Buddha nature becomes a concrete, lived, reality.
- There is indeed a real question of whether a person who desires enlightenment so ardently can ever acquire it, since by definition Buddhahood is complete desirelessness. In fact, **Dogen Zenji says that all that is required is simple faith in one's intrinsic Buddha nature.**
- A contemporary Zen master has said that **"Zen is picking up your coat from the floor and hanging it up."**that is all that is required, and nothing could be simpler. Yet how difficult!
- ... the alert and receptive mind reflects all events clearly and without distortion. ... we do not correlate the experience with some idea, mainly "good" or "bad".
- So this practice is simple, but it is difficult. **The difficulty lies on not adding something extra to the events of our life.**
- ... **unless a person has a very real commitment to following the Way and understands this commitment as a total response to all events and activities, these ordinary activities will be a real burden.**

### Two – The Importance of Faith

- There is not, in other words, a time for the Christian when faith is no longer important, for the tenets of his religion are never experientially validated in the way the doctrines of Buddhism are.
- There is, for the Buddhist, therefore, a vast difference between believing all things are impermanent, for instance, and knowing they are.
- Buddhist faith is a very deep certitude in the veracity of a certain doctrine, accepted and used as a touchstone for conduct in the faith that practice will verify its truth.
- **Buddhism teaches that the faith will eventually be replaced by knowledge and that any teaching not verifiable in this way ought to be rejected.**
- ... the faith amounts to a kind of certainty because of the anticipation of validation.
- **To have faith in the Buddha and his teaching and to commit oneself without reservation to hard practice is atonement.**
- Dogen's Zen is the Zen of faith.
- **The direct pursuit of enlightenment is a confession of dualistic thinking** and merely one more attempt to seek self-gratification, and both of these are self-defeating.
- To have faith in the Buddha is the same as forgetting the self.
- True zazen, then, is any activity carried out without self-concern, not forming self-serving attitudes towards events, and **living one's ordinary life without attachment or loathing**.

### Three – Arousing the Thought of Enlightenment

- ... the search does not really begin until the thought of enlightenment has occurred. With the commitment, there is no practice, and without practice, there is no attainment.
- In Dogen Zenji's writing, **the thought of enlightenment is closely bound up with the perception of impermanence**.
- ... is any kind of progress in self-betterment possible as long as practice is primarily undertaken with the primarily selfish desire to improve one's own lot? ... The Mahayana Buddhist answer to this question has been "no".
- **the Bodhisattva' slow progress towards final, complete enlightenment is the result of this other-directed activity**.
- In making the vow, he destines himself to be forever excluded from the ultimate goal.
- ... if any being enters the Dharma for themself, and not for others, is is not the Dharma, and they have not entered.

#### Four – The Problem of Karma

- Zen Buddhism is not Buddhist psychotherapy, ... but is the Buddhist means of dealing with karma and its consequences.
- The psychological insight of Buddhism is that we are unable to perform [volitional] acts without their leaving some influence in our lives.
- **Buddhism** is truly a human religion, because it **does not attempt to distract us from the reality of sentient existence** by promising a future nirvana or heaven which is exempt from **the conditions of sentient life**.
- **Instead of vainly seeking to escape the conditions which constitute our lives** by means of flight ... one **find a real and meaningful freedom** from this conditionedness **through a radical, profound affirmation of one's very conditionedness**.
- ... **one identifies one hundred percent with the circumstance**.
- "When on side is illuminated," says Dogen, "the other side is darkened." ... **completely, with nothing added, nothing but.**
- ... it **immerses the individual even more deeply into the flow of wordly existence**, **affirming and transforming worldly experience itself**.  
- Zen training shows us first of all what we really are, our real personality as distinct from the projected idealisation. Then, **with luck and hard work, we discover that what we really are is good enough and has been all along.**

### Five – The Scriptures


- Bodhidharma: "no dependence on words or letters" - no dependence, not don't read or don't use.
- A careful study of the scriptures can enhance and strengthen our central practice.
- Zen practice itself has as its goal **the overcoming of a false dualism** whereby we tend to discriminate between things which are **worthy and unworthy, sacred and profane, good and bad**, Buddha and non-Buddha, and so on.
- Many bad ideas, prejudice, baseless assumptions and other forms of ignorance can be removed by means of better information and calm, clear, thinking.
- Zen practice is ... to **free and activate the total individual, with all his talents, functions, and abilities**, and **this includes the mind**, as well as all **the feelings**, **the physical organism**, and the will.
- **Service to others is the object of Zen practice.**
