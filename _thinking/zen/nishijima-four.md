---

> While studying the Shōbōgenzō, Nishijima developed a theory he called "**three philosophies and one reality**,"[6] which presents his distinctive interpretation of the Four Noble Truths as well as explaining the structure of Dōgen's writing. ...
> - The first perspective is "**idealist**," "abstract," "spiritual," and "**subjective**"; Nishijima says this is the correct interpretation of the first Noble Truth (in mainstream Buddhism, the first Noble Truth is dukkha).
> - The second perspective is "**concrete**", "materialistic", "scientific", and "**objective**" (in mainstream Buddhism, samudaya).
> - The third perspective is described as an **integration** of the first two, producing a "**realistic**" synthesis (mainstream, nirodha).
> - The fourth perspective is **reality** itself, which Nishijima argues **cannot be contained in philosophy or stated in words**, but which Dōgen attempts to suggest through poetry and symbolism. In mainstream Buddhism, the fourth Noble Truth is the Noble Eightfold Path.
> – [Gudō Wafu Nishijima on Wikipeda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gud%C5%8D_Wafu_Nishijima)

- the fourth view points, not to an abstract philosophy, but to reality itself
- The Theory of Four Views, rather than The Four Noble Truths
- In Shobogenzo, Dogen expresses his ideas in four phases: idealistic (abstract concepts); objective, material, concrete; as a real problem, realistically thinking, by bringing together the subjective and object, by synthesising the viewpoints into a realistic appraisal of the problem, of the self and the external world; suggest the ineffable nature of reality itself by using symbolic, poetic, figurative speech.
- Theoretical, objective, realistic, figurative or poetic
- Buddha's discovery was that we are living in reality, not just in the world of ideas or just in the world of matter. But philosophy is not reality, so we need a method for people to see the nature of reality directly: that's zazen.
- The four truths as idealism, materialism, realism, reality itself.
