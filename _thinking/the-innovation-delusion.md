## Raw notes

> Never set out to innovate, because more horror is done with that goal in mind than any other. – Charles Eames

> Another flaw in the human character is that everybody wants to build and nobody wants to do the maintenance. – Kurt Vonnegut

### Chapter 1: The Problem With Innovation

- "Move fast and break things" ... works in the digital economy where ... the cost of fixing broken code pales in comparison to the costs of fixing a physical product.
- As business leaders embraced this worldview, its effects spilled out beyond the economy. We adjusted out values, even our vision of democracy, to be suitably deferential to the gods of Silicon Valley.
- ... while ... "Move fast and break things" is still considered good counsel for web designers and app builders – professions where profit margins are high, the costs of failure are low, and venture capital is plentiful – it turns out that "move fast and break things," and the innovation mindset more generally, can be lousy guidance for anyone who builds or designs actual things.
- When digital-age companies encounter old problems in their new ventures in the material world – logistics, manufacturing, consumer tastes, societal norms and regulations – they consistently flounder.
- Innovation-speak is fundamentally dishonest. While it is often cast in terms of optimism, talking of opportunity and creativity and a boundless future, it is in fact the rhetoric of fear. It plays on our worry that we will be left behind.
- ... innovation often functions as a proxy for values perceived to be lacking in society – the most common example being when someone proposes techno-solutions to profound social problems (such as racism or poverty).
- At its most extreme, innovation-speak actively devalues the work of most humans, especially those who do the dirty work that keeps our technological civilisation running.
- ... it fails to capture the essence of human life with technology – where maintenance and reliability are far more valuable than innovation and disruption.

### Chapter 2: Turning Anxiety into a Product

- Humanity has not always cherished innovation, or even progress.
- ... Confucius framed his work not as a new invention stemming from his authorial genius, but as a a codification of traditional ideas and values ...
- When we consider the broad and epp history of human thought, our contemporary obsession with the new looks downright odd.
- ... its users assume that social progress comes from the introduction of new things – even if there is a short-term harm ...
- ... observers preferred stories of individual genius to the more complex reality, a preference that continues to this day.
- ... some of the biggest social advancements of the last two hundred years – such as the abolition of slavery or the expansion of voting rights to women and minorities – had little to do with technological change.
- ... creating illusory crises is a common habit among innovation-speakers.
- ... by the mid-1990s, observers noted that economic inequality had increased during this period of supposedly rapid technological change – not everyone had shared in innovation's bounty.
- Who should be trusted as an "expert"? ... people who have demonstrated efficacy
- Wittgenstein's "the craving for generality" – we often hanker for universalities in cases where the complex world evades such easy summary.

### Chapter 3: Technology after Innovation

- Thought experiment
    1. What technologies are around you? Are they old or new?
    2. Which technologies are essential for your well-being, and which could you do without?
    3. Recall the technology you used in the past 24 hours. Where they old or new?
    - The goal is to draw attention to how widespread ordinary technologies are in your everyday life.
- ... much of human history is ... stories of stability: of how societies coordinate labour to maintain the large-scale public systems we've relied of since ancient times
- Technology (as an object) isn't merely digital and new; and technology (as a process) isn't merely innovation.
- Ursula K. Le Guin: "Technology is how a society copes with physical reality"
- A piece of technology passes through three basic phases: innovation, maintenance, decay.
- Mierle Laderman Ukele, Carol Gilligan, Nel Noddings, Virginia Held: care is fundamental for all societies; it is frequently feminised; it is usually undervalued and underpaid; it appears in all social settings.
- When things go wrong, the first place we should look is to see if the relations of care are healthy.
- Cultural expecations – including senses of cleanliness, order, and duty – play an enormous role is determining what kinds of upkeep we choose to do.
- While the new technologies spared women from the more physically demanding side of tasks – like the more gruelling aspects of laundry – they led to higher standards of cleanliness and women doing more housework, work that was by definition never finished,
- Two important concepts emerged early in the twentieth century: deferred maintenance and preventative maintenance.
- This instinct – to pin all of our hopes on innovation – is exactly the problem that we summarise as The Innovation Delusion.
