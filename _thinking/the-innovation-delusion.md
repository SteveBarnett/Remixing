## Raw notes

> Never set out to innovate, because more horror is done with that goal in mind than any other. – Charles Eames

> Another flaw in the human character is that everybody wants to build and nobody wants to do the maintenance. – Kurt Vonnegut

### Chapter 1: The Problem With Innovation

- "Move fast and break things" ... works in the digital economy where ... the cost of fixing broken code pales in comparison to the costs of fixing a physical product.
- As business leaders embraced this worldview, its effects spilled out beyond the economy. We adjusted out values, even our vision of democracy, to be suitably deferential to the gods of Silicon Valley.
- ... while ... "Move fast and break things" is still considered good counsel for web designers and app builders – professions where profit margins are high, the costs of failure are low, and venture capital is plentiful – it turns out that "move fast and break things," and the innovation mindset more generally, can be lousy guidance for anyone who builds or designs actual things.
- When digital-age companies encounter old problems in their new ventures in the material world – logistics, manufacturing, consumer tastes, societal norms and regulations – they consistently flounder.
- Innovation-speak is fundamentally dishonest. While it is often cast in terms of optimism, talking of opportunity and creativity and a boundless future, it is in fact the rhetoric of fear. It plays on our worry that we will be left behind.
- ... innovation often functions as a proxy for values perceived to be lacking in society – the most common example being when someone proposes techno-solutions to profound social problems (such as racism or poverty).
- At its most extreme, innovation-speak actively devalues the work of most humans, especially those who do the dirty work that keeps our technological civilisation running.
- ... it fails to capture the essence of human life with technology – where maintenance and reliability are far more valuable than innovation and disruption.

### Chapter 2: Turning Anxiety into a Product

- Humanity has not always cherished innovation, or even progress.
- ... Confucius framed his work not as a new invention stemming from his authorial genius, but as a a codification of traditional ideas and values ...
- When we consider the broad and epp history of human thought, our contemporary obsession with the new looks downright odd.
- ... its users assume that social progress comes from the introduction of new things – even if there is a short-term harm ...
- ... observers preferred stories of individual genius to the more complex reality, a preference that continues to this day.
- ... some of the biggest social advancements of the last two hundred years – such as the abolition of slavery or the expansion of voting rights to women and minorities – had little to do with technological change.
- ... creating illusory crises is a common habit among innovation-speakers.
- ... by the mid-1990s, observers noted that economic inequality had increased during this period of supposedly rapid technological change – not everyone had shared in innovation's bounty.
- Who should be trusted as an "expert"? ... people who have demonstrated efficacy
- Wittgenstein's "the craving for generality" – we often hanker for universalities in cases where the complex world evades such easy summary.

### Chapter 3: Technology after Innovation

- Thought experiment
    1. What technologies are around you? Are they old or new?
    2. Which technologies are essential for your well-being, and which could you do without?
    3. Recall the technology you used in the past 24 hours. Where they old or new?
    - The goal is to draw attention to how widespread ordinary technologies are in your everyday life.
- ... much of human history is ... stories of stability: of how societies coordinate labour to maintain the large-scale public systems we've relied of since ancient times
- Technology (as an object) isn't merely digital and new; and technology (as a process) isn't merely innovation.
- Ursula K. Le Guin: "Technology is how a society copes with physical reality"
- A piece of technology passes through three basic phases: innovation, maintenance, decay.
- Mierle Laderman Ukele, Carol Gilligan, Nel Noddings, Virginia Held: care is fundamental for all societies; it is frequently feminised; it is usually undervalued and underpaid; it appears in all social settings.
- When things go wrong, the first place we should look is to see if the relations of care are healthy.
- Cultural expecations – including senses of cleanliness, order, and duty – play an enormous role is determining what kinds of upkeep we choose to do.
- While the new technologies spared women from the more physically demanding side of tasks – like the more gruelling aspects of laundry – they led to higher standards of cleanliness and women doing more housework, work that was by definition never finished,
- Two important concepts emerged early in the twentieth century: deferred maintenance and preventative maintenance.
- This instinct – to pin all of our hopes on innovation – is exactly the problem that we summarise as The Innovation Delusion.

### Chapter 4: Slow Disaster

- Deferred maintenance: work put off until some later fantasy date when there would be more resources
- The Metro's problems were compounded by a mindset that favours superficial ideas of innovation and growth.
- The historian Scott Knowles has created a helpful term for describing these situations: slow disaster. Fast disasters, or what we normally call disasters, include hurricanes, flooding, tornadoes, earthquakes, industrial accidents
- A slow disaster, by contrast, is the accretion of harm from incremental neglect.
- Slow disasters can lead to fast disasters, of course.
- The notion of slow disasters brings the issue of deferred maintenance and the way we prioritise new systems into focus, in part because it helps us to see these stories in the long view.
- Blaming bad events on moral failure is easy pickings, but often it's not the best explanation when things go wrong.
- What degrading infrastructure makes clear is that economic growth is not unidirectional.
- The consequences lie over the horizon, however, so officials and citizens can congratulate themselves for accomplishing something while leaving the worries and problems to the future.
- ... see things like Taleb: avoid overplanning and choose simple but resilient solutions.
- If governments, organisations, and individuals build and buy systems without providing for their future care, we end up facing a stress-inducing mountain of deferred maintenance and infrastructural debt.
- ... this neglect levels disproportionate costs on populations that have already borne the brunt of systemic patterns of social and economic inequality.
- Our infrastructural choices are marked by a kind of naive, even dangerous, optimism that the people of tomorrow will somehow be able to pay for structures we build today.
- ... these domains struggle with maintenance mainly because they obsess over growth and short-tern gains instead of taking care of what they have.

### Chapter 5: Growth at All Costs

- Silicon Valley's "fail fast" mantra has limits as a general model
- "Disruption" can cause pain and damage in people's lives without delivering the promised results
- Leaders should not lose sight of the fundamental importance of maintenance, reliability, and the hard work it takes to keep things running smoothly and safely
- the preponderance of superficial ideas of innovation and growth
- the political risks involved with (responsible) investments in maintenance
- the fact that neglecting maintenance often brings disproportionate harm to people already grappling with social and economic disadvantages
- The gospel of growth is a part of economic orthodoxy, deeply ingrained in all varieties of industrial capitalism
- [Measure like GDP] ignore values that aren't easily measured and narrow the aperture for appreaciating things that make life worth living
- The growth delusion persists because humans instinctively revert to short-term thinking and respond to simple, short-term incentives
- It's important to emphasise that these executives and managers aren't evil and they aren't dupes. Rather, they are trapped in situations in which their primary duty is to ensure that investors and shareholders are happy – in other words that the corporation creates ever-increasing returns on investment
- Economists William Lazonick and Mary O'Sullivan have shown how companies that fixate on growth have a way of undermining the factors that contribute to long-term success, such as investments in research and development or employee wages and benefits.
- If the goal of innovative companies is to constantly increase profit, then anything and everything is fair game for being put in service of that cause – even values like efficiency and sustainability.

### Chapter 6: The Maintainer Caste

- Within organisations and society at large, maintenance roles often fall at the bottom of status hierarchies.
- Over time, in Western culture, ... the distinction between work of the "head" and and work of the "hand": mental labour was seen as belonging to a higher order than manual labour, which was done by individuals in the lower ranks.
- Most innovations are incremental and come from individuals who are subject matter experts and who apply themselves to improving the domain they know so well. There is no shortcut to decades of training and hard work.
- The myth of the necessity of college education encourages us to think that the job that pays the most money is somehow the best job for us, when in reality people find more joy, meaning, and pleasure doing work that suits them.
- Maintainers are often introverts. ... [they] can find it hard to advocate for themselves and their labours.
- The job of auto mechanic is usually considered to be fairly low in status, but when a car breaks, the power dynamic shifts.
- Being a maintainer comes with real social and economic costs.
- A lack of recognition and a lack of compensation.
- Cleaners experience invisibility at work and invisibility of work
- Picture the job market as a kind of pyramid, with the very few innovators sitting at the top being supported by a very broad base of maintainers.

### Chapter 7: A Crisis of Care

- The vast majority of people work as maintainers, but nearly all of us are maintainers at home.
- Bodily maintenance and care may seem quite different from upkeep focused on technologies, but we've found that that same basic problems affect both forms, including discounting a growth mindset, and a failure to address widespread, collective problems.
- It's hard to encourage self-maintenance in a world that is literally designed to appeal to our worst selves.
- Corporations have purposely made it even more difficult to repair appliances and electronic devices than it is to fix our homes.
- One of the most remarkable things about our society is how relatively few of our broken possesions most of us will repair.
- Small businesses, individual consumers, and environmental sustainability are all negatively impacted by repair restrictions.
- Right-to-repair advocates also highlight environmental sustainability and the value of community as important causes.
