## Events

- I did a solo [rōhatsu](/thinking/zen/rohatsu-2025/) again.
- I read a few takes on Dōgen's Instructions to the Cook.
- I read a few books more broadly on Dōgen.
- My dog Roxy was old and in pain, so I read a bunch about pain and death and Zen.
- I've continued to sit almost every day, zazen-style.

## Study

- I circled around One Bright Pearl in a couple of ways, ending up with [One Bright Flow](/thinking/one-bright-flow/) as a fruitful way for me to cosider it.
    - The feeling of everything showing up together all at once
    - The feeling of happy, playful, bright activity
    - The feeling of non-doing, wu wei, happening
- A topic that recurred was not chasing enlightenment. Just sitting, and doing what needs to be done.
- I dug into [emptiness](/thinking/empty/), feeling my way into a bit more.
- Not needing to have a calm mind. But/and not chasing after thoughts.
- It's been helping with self-acceptance.

## Some plucked notes

- All things are impermanent 
- All things are interdependent 
- Humans are part of nature
- The non-duality of samsara and nirvana
- nonduality - not a negation of both monism and dualism, a dynamic interaction of them
- Insight and wisdom are lived experiences that cannot be encapsulated in words 
- Buddha-nature is something that must be lived moment to moment
- The ethics of Zen are contextual rather than absolute
- The aim of a koan is to precipitate a genuine religious crisis. They work by inducing extreme tension-in-doubt.
- Zen frees us to make free and lively use of ethics and common sense rather than be constrained and limited by them. We can push past the habits and patterns of what we think works for our lives.
- Zen is in the ordinary details of life
- To talk about The Way is to miss it
- Not-knowing is the state of openness, leaving space, being open to possibilities 
- Practice itself is the goal 
- Freedom as in improvisation, not as in unconstrained randomness
- A person is a manifestation of their combined activity, a process 
- Emptiness is the complete interdependence of all things.
- Great awakening is the enlightenment that goes beyond delusion and enlightenment.
- Pain is part of life. It’s not separate, an enemy, or a failure.
- For most of us, death will be long and slow. An unravelling, decay, and disintegration.
- Waking up is a release from the need to die a good death.
- Our attempts to find fixed, static, lasting states are at odds with the dynamic, processual, reality of everything and everyone 
- We can’t get rid of thoughts. Thinking thoughts is what the mind does.
- Make friends with yourself as you are, not as you wish you are, or think you out to be, honestly and kindly.
- Equanimity means calm, centred, acceptance of everything in our reality 
    - Over time our practice usually gives us more equanimity 
- “The mind of before-knowing” – Dogen’s phrasing of don’t-know mind
- The more we think about Reality the further we go from it
    - Don’t speculate about life and the world. Live it!
- Zen operates in the concrete and real, the down to earth 
- As we practice we update the way we see ourselves, our self-identity
- Feeling pent-up emotions releases the energy being employed to repress them
- Recognize and accept unwanted emotions as possible life reactions
- The strategy is to come as close to your discomfort as is possible without actually being overwhelmed or bolting to escape the discomfort
- Our brain processes discomfort as danger, as something to get rid of.
- The oneness of practice and enlightenment.
- The relative (difference and particularity) and absolute (everything is a seamless whole) aspects of reality aren’t really separate
- We can find refuge by embracing and transforming suffering rather than running away from it. 
- Free yourself from the habit of turning away from every little agitation that arises
- Learn to accept and be with the wounds we carry rather than rushing to try and heal them.
- Your like or dislike isn’t going to change a bad situation. It’s what you do that matters. So being able to put aside your preferences and act is much more useful than worrying about what you like or don’t like.
- There’s a difference between your mind’s natural tendency to wander and getting lost in the wanderings of your mind.
- The fact that you’re a little more stable because of your practice is something you give to the world.
