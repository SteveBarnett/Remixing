- Consider recent and current applications to test usability
- It's mostly about being critical of AI, but also more generally about good decision-making

## Raw notes

- Look at the wider picture and see what we’re missing

- Keep Negative Capability and be able to argue both sides of the argument

- Focus on the know problems, and offer good alternatives, time-tested and trusted

- Our strength as workers comes from our ability to coordinate and collaborate.
- We seek to be empowered and entrusted to do our jobs effectively.

- What resources are being invested in AI, and to whose benefit?
- What do you think these AI advocates see as the goal or value of increased AI adoption?
- How might investment in AI be antithetical to what we want from our workplace?

- Are workers’ needs best met via AI or something else?
- You recognise when a task isn’t right for AI. Consider whether the task involves personal relationships or high-stakes decisions
- You consider what AI does well and where it struggles. Good at: generating options, structure. Bad at: nuance and tone, specific facts, being concise.

- You stay involved rather than just accepting what AI produces. Request options instead of one answer.


- Ask for an “AI implementation committee” – a group of workers able to meet and speak freely without management
    - Motivate it as a way to ensure productivity, effectiveness, correctness, rather than as a challenge to profitability

- AI has no in-built braking mechanism to stop you from over-relying on AI. 
- It’s often assumed the Amish don’t use any electricity, but in fact, what most Amish actually avoid is the electrical grid. The distinction is subtle but important.
- Avoiding AI where it threatens our cognitive, relational, or creative skills, safeguards the ways we are human
- The Sacred Triad: Cognition, Relationships, Spirit

- Refuse to compete on the machines’ terms
- Leave our human mark on the things we create
- Prioritise human originality and human effort

- Start with values, not technology.
- Start with the goal, not technology.
- Start with the problem, not the solution.

- Innovation-speak is often cast as optimism and opportunity and creativity, but it’s actually playing on FOMO.
    - Creating illusory crises is a common habit among innovation-speakers

- When things go wrong, the first place we should look is to see if the relations of care are healthy 

- avoid overplanning and choose simple but resilient solutions

- Leaders should not lose sight of the fundamental importance of maintenance, reliability, and the hard work it takes to keep things running smoothly and safely

- The Innovation Delusion is the false belief that the pursuit of innovation and novelty will lead us into the promised land of growth and profit when, in reality, it will lead us to ignore the ever-accumulating pile of deferred maintenance and infrastructural debt – and in the process, lead individuals toward burnout and our society to accelerating levels of exploitation and inequality.

- Spend more time talking about how these systems benefit and harm human life

- We can do things the hacker way: resistance using technology, rather than avoiding it.

- Focusing on quality rather than quantity reduces workloads and makes work more pleasant.

- Technology developed by capitalism furthers its goals: we work more and have less autonomy.

- Instead of daydreaming about an imaginary post-work utopia, we can speak to what is and can learn from historical struggles.

- Bring out and work with the underlying values, needs, emotions, identities. 

- State your assumptions, admit your areas of ignorance.

- Make a simple request for the need. Tell people your expectations, ask theirs.

- Recognise the partialness, incompleteness, of any viewpoint.

- “I don’t know.” - shows trust and honesty.

- Build rather than attack.
    - Offer good alternatives, drawn from time-tested traditions, tools, and process.

- When working with people, especially on accessibility things, I try my best to: be empathetic; make it simple; be positive.

- Use “both/and”.
- Assume best intent, attend to impact.
- Focus on strategies rather issues.

- The price of a successful attack is a constructive alternative

- Engage tension, but don’t indulge drama.

- Flip it to test it.
- Offer alternatives, drawn from time-tested traditions, tools, and process.

- Ask: who it does it for, and who it does it to (rather than what it does).
- Choose: where, when, and how to use automation.
- Switch: from fighting technologies to fighting bosses.

- Watch out for criti-hype: criticism that both feeds and feeds on hype. 

- Since juniors using AI aren’t solving hard problems, they aren’t getting the experience needed to become seniors.

- AI sells itself as a way to cut wage costs by replacing humans with AI. Anything else is secondary. 
- There are no AI value propositions that say “We can help you charge your customers less and pay your workers more.” 

- Remember that every situation is workable
- Take the long view
- See actions in relation to paths of least resistance, systems, root causes
- Be generous and vulnerable to make your connections open and durable
- Keep as much intact as possible for everyone

- Make noise, be seen, break the silence
- Do what’s right, not what’s easy.
- Be willing to be blunt, to upset people.
- Work with tension, but don’t indulge drama


- Be a sceptic. Ask lots of questions using a critical lens. In particular: ask for the details and specifics.
- Be a Cynic. Choose thoughtfully if and when to use AI. Sometimes choose not to use AI at all.
- Be a Luddite. (Also) Highlight the cons, downsides, and harms of using AI. And: offer good, time-tested, alternatives.

- Read calls to use AI as a sales pitch. 

- Notice how the real breakthrough is always just around the corner.

- Put the burden of proof of effectiveness on the AI systems. 

- a human-centred process is more important than an AI-centred product.
