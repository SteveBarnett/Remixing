"New" is not a value proposition. But we tend to fall for it. Our brains are hard-wired to seek novelty, so we tend to associate "new" with "good".

I saw an advert for a drink the other day. The sum total of the words on it were "New! Try it now.". To me, this seemed a bit odd. There's only *What* here, no *Why*. Nothing about how delicious it is. Or how it will give me more energy, or make more relaxed. Just "New." And then a command: "Try it now".

We could argue that there was limited space so they only had a few words. But even then, this feels like a friend of "Show Don't Tell". There's no attempt at convincing, or of telling me, or even really of selling me the drink. Just a demand for consumer compliance.

"New" is not a value proposition. One big reason is that it will soon no longer be true. New quickly degrades into the weaker Newest, then into Regular and eventually into Old.

Close cousins of "New" as a sales pitch are "More" and "Faster". Like "New," these often do not stand up to close scrutiny.

- "More" is not always good. Having to do more work in the same amount of time is not good. More food than I can eat leads to waste, which is not good. More lines of code is not good: we want just enough to get the job done. More choices are not often good: we can get overwhelmed and have analysis paralysis.
- "Faster" is not always good. Finishing a piece of work faster usually means making a compromise somewhere: either quality or scope. Faster is not good for music: a short song isn't better than a long one; playing a song at 10 times speed is not better than playing it at 1 times speed.

Products that only offer "New" (or "More", or "Faster") as their main sales pitch are worth poking at it. What's the actual value proposition here?
