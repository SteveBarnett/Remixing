- We come to see that without grounding in presence, our care can too easily turn into burnout, reactivity, or despair.
- Our practice isn’t separate from action—it’s what makes our loving action possible.
- Calling on the depth of our presence and care, we can learn to transform demons of anger, shame, fear, and hatred into spirit guides.

### Part one: the ground of presence

- This section offers foundational practices that awaken embodied awareness. You will learn to pause, to observe the mind without judgment, and to return to the aliveness of breath and body.

#### Arriving in presence

- We awaken by meeting the present moment with unconditional presence.
- The way back is through shifting attention from mental activity to the sensory aliveness of our immediate experience
- With each fresh arrival you are deepening your capacity for homecoming.
- Let your intention be to stay here in living presence, offering a relaxed attention to this changing play of sensations, feelings, sound, and breath. If you are drawn into your thoughts, gently return to your senses.

#### The inner pause

- Between the stimulus and the response, there is a space, and in that space is your power and your freedom. — Widely attributed to Viktor Frankl
- In the stillness of the pause, we step out of reactivity and reconnect with the deeper truth of our being.
- Make a weeklong commitment to explore the inner pause throughout your day during normal activities— during transitions, before responding to a text or email, while waiting in line, or as you begin a meal.
- Were there certain moments in your day when it felt especially difficult—or especially easeful—to pause? What made the difference?

#### The power of yes

- By Yes I mean a fundamental acceptance of life in the here and now.
- If instead we soften that resistance—even slightly—and open to what is actually here, we tap into a deeper source of wisdom and compassion. This Yes is not about agreement or approval.
- Yes is an honest and brave acknowledgment of what is true—an acceptance of what is within us and around us.
- How might your experience shift—in the hours, weeks, or months to come—if you could bring the spirit of Yes to the inevitable challenges and sorrows of life?

#### Mindful body scan

- As you scan your body during this practice, resolve to meet whatever arises with curiosity and kindness.

#### Mindful of breath

- The particular sensations, emotions, or thoughts that arise when we practice mindfulness are not so important. The point is to cultivate the willingness to become still and pay attention to our experience, whatever it may be.

#### Mindful of thoughts

- One of the most powerful shifts in our healing and awakening comes when we begin to recognize that we are not our thoughts.
- These thoughts create a virtual reality that keeps us disconnected from the aliveness of the present moment.
- If a thought pulls you in, that’s okay. The moment you notice, you’ve already stepped back into presence. Gently acknowledge, and begin again.
- What did you discover about your relationship with your thoughts? Did you notice any particular beliefs or inner voices that tend to shape your experience?

### Part two: embracing our inner life

- You’ll also explore turning toward the vulnerable places you may have long avoided—the wounds, fears, shame, and loneliness. Rather than armoring yourself against them, you will learn to meet them with compassion and presence.
- This is not about fixing yourself but about recognizing your own worthiness and the radiant awareness beneath all experience.

#### Awakening self-kindness

- We suffer when we identify with these coverings—our fear-based habits—and forget the gold, our essential goodness.
- because we tend to fixate on what feels wrong, we do need to commit to reflecting on our goodness and offering ourselves care.
- As you sense what matters to your heart and what you aspire toward, recognize this as an expression of basic goodness.

#### When self-kindness feels out of reach

- There are times when self-judgment is so entrenched, so deeply grooved, that offering yourself kindness feels impossible—or even phony.
- If this resonates, please experiment with these gentle ways of supporting this practice
- Remember your intention. Simply acknowledging that you want to be kind is powerful.
- Make a gesture of care—even if it feels mechanical. Place a hand on your heart. Offer yourself a kind wish or phrase. Sometimes the gesture itself begins to awaken a sense of warmth.
- Call to mind someone who has loved you well. Whether it’s a friend, a teacher, or a beloved pet, let their care wash over you.
- Write yourself a short, encouraging note. Something you can read again and again.
- These tiny acts plant seeds of compassion that, over time, take root.

#### The RAIN meditation and difficult emotions

- Now bring to mind a situation in your life that regularly evokes emotional reactivity—such as fear, anger, hurt, shame, guilt, sadness, or confusion. It might relate to a relationship, your work, health, or an area of addictive or unhealthy behavior.
- As you bring your attention to whatever feels triggering or disturbing, ask yourself, “What’s the worst part of this?” Notice what arises—in your mind and in your body—and stay present with the feelings as they unfold.
- What do you notice when your heart leans toward allowing?
- With interested and kind attention, deepen your exploration
- “What does this part of me most need?”
- Calling on the wisest and most compassionate part of your being, explore offering care to your inner life
- If you find it difficult to offer yourself compassion, imagine someone wise and loving—a friend, child, dog, healer, teacher, spiritual figure, tree, or ancestor—regarding you with care and offering a compassionate message or reminder.
- In After the RAIN, we shift from doing to being. The invitation is to relax and let go into the heartspace that has emerged. Rest in this awareness and become familiar with it; this is your true home.

#### The self-forgiveness scan

- It’s an especially cleansing way to end the day.
- sense whether self-blame or judgment is keeping you from your own heart.
- Pause and notice what arises—in your body, in your thoughts. Are there stories of falling short? Of letting others down? Of not meeting your own standards as a parent, partner, or friend—or simply as a human being?
- Imagine the inner freedom of recognizing your imperfections and holding them with acceptance and kindness.
- imagine who you are when you live with a forgiving heart—free, open, connected

#### RAIN of self-forgiveness

- Many of us carry deep conditioning that tells us something is fundamentally wrong with us. We fixate on our human imperfections, seeing them as proof of unworthiness, and gradually build a strong armor of self- aversion around our hearts.
- Allow the reality of this moment: that you’ve turned against yourself.
- Notice your posture—how does your body express the sense of “something’s wrong with me”?
- How does this hurting place want you to be with it? What does this hurting place need?
- What would help it feel comforted, understood, forgiven?
- If you imagine a loving, trusted presence—a person, spiritual figure, ancestor, deity—that is offering you forgiveness … what words or energy would most matter?
- Notice your heart … Who are you without the belief that something is wrong with you?

#### Examining self-beliefs

- It is only when we directly open ourselves to the suffering of this trance—how it cuts us off from others and from our own heart and spirit, how we don’t have to believe we’re flawed—that we begin to intuit the freedom possible in shedding old skin.
- “What am I believing about myself?”
- Our negative self-beliefs, even when deeply painful, often give us a sense of certainty, orientation, and control.
- “What is wrong with letting go of this belief?” or “What bad might happen if I let go of this self-judgment?”

#### RAIN for releasing painful beliefs

- the Investigation step is especially focused on unpacking and loosening the hold of beliefs that create suffering.
- Bring to mind a belief that causes suffering in your life. To connect fully with this belief, you might remind yourself of a particular situation that brought it up—or one that is likely to.
- Your thoughts and feelings express a belief. What are you believing right now?
- Begin by asking, “Is this really true?” or “Am I certain this is true?”
- Are particular emotions associated with this belief? Do you sense fear or shame, anger or self-hatred?
- Widen the Investigation by asking, “How has living with this belief affected my life?” Can you see its impact on how you relate to yourself and others, on your creativity, your capacity to serve, your ability to enjoy experience, your inner growth?
- At this point, you might pause and ask yourself, “What is it like to honestly see and feel how this belief has shaped my life?”

### Part three: loving without holding back in relationships

- The training grounds are our day-to-day relationships. This is where we learn to listen, speak from the heart, deepen empathy, stay present in the face of conflict, and recognize the basic goodness in those around us.

#### Listening with the ear of the heart

- Mindful listening is a gift of presence. It invites us to pause our inner monologue and open fully to another.
- At its heart, engaged listening is a practice of surrender—letting go of control and opening to the person before us, just as they are.
- Intention: “I want to connect, not separate.”

#### Courage to express vulnerability

- Deepening the truths we tell each other is not about dramatic revelations; it’s about gradually removing the layers of protection that keep us from being fully seen.
- This kind of truth-sharing is an act of care.
- Ask, “What is my deeper intention in sharing this?”
- Ask, “Am I offering this truth to create more closeness, understanding, or trust?”
- SPEAK FROM VULNERABILITY, NOT FROM ANALYSES OR ABSTRACTION Use “I” statements and share your own experience.
- CREATE SPACE FOR THE OTHER’S TRUTH Connection deepens when both people feel safe enough to express what is vulnerable and true.
- Deep truth often unfolds over time. Let go of needing one conversation to fix or cover everything.
- What truth feels important for you to share in a current relationship—but remains unspoken? Why?
- Let the conversation be a beginning, not a final sharing.

#### Speaking difficult truths with mindfulness

- Difficult truths are honest expressions that feel uncomfortable to share because they reveal something tender, painful, or potentially disruptive in a relationship. They might involve unmet needs, boundaries, past hurts, or deeper feelings we’ve been holding back.
- The challenge—and the practice—is to express what is real and true in a way that invites understanding and
connection, not separation.
- It’s about discerning what is useful, timely, and kind. And it’s about cultivating the humility to recognize that the perception of truth, in human relationships, is often shaped by our conditioning—layered, imperfect, and evolving.
- “What do I imagine will be the impact of what I share? Does this match my intention?”
- Is this a good time for you—and for the other person—to have this conversation?
- Begin by naming your intention: to deepen connection, build trust, or share something that feels important to your relationship. This helps the other person feel your care and lowers their defensiveness.
- Leave space for the other person’s perspective. Truths are usually layered and relative—shaped by our unique experiences and perceptions.
- Difficult truths often need space to land.
- What are you afraid might happen if you speak it?
- How have you felt in the past when someone told you a difficult truth with care?

#### Compassion for dear ones in difficulty

- It’s not our habit to truly attend to the fragility of others, because so much of our energy is devoted to protecting our own vulnerability. Yet when we intentionally train ourselves to see beneath the surface—to look past the masks and sense the wounds and fears others carry—our habitual armoring begins to soften.
- We begin to recognize our shared humanity. Vulnerability is not a personal flaw—it is a universal truth. This recognition helps dissolve the shame that keeps us feeling separate and unworthy.
- Let your basic inquiries be “What’s it like being you?” and “Where does it hurt?”
- How might you bring more compassion into your daily interactions—especially with those you take for granted or judge?

#### RAIN for conflict

- It’s not that we are fundamentally in conflict with another person—it’s that our needs, values, or perceptions are momentarily in tension with theirs. When we personalize conflict, we often become reactive, defensive, or disconnected.
- If we can hold both our own experience and the other person’s with care, a wiser path forward often reveals itself—one that can not only bridge the divide but deepen connection.
- rest in a sense of mutual vulnerability and shared longing for understanding and connection.

#### A forgiving heart

- Eric Kolvig.
- “I allow myself to be a learner, still learning life’s lessons.”
- Trust that even the intention to forgive plants seeds of healing.

#### Healing separation - from head to heartspace

- Our minds are deeply conditioned to create separation—from others and from ourselves—through constant
storytelling: who’s right or wrong, good or bad, to blame or to praise.
- Even subtle resentments or unspoken judgments can erode connection for days or decades.
- Observe how thoughts and stories tend to reinforce separation.
- What might help you remember and reconnect to heartspace when judgment or reactivity arises?

#### Others help us remember the gold

- We come to know who we are through the reflections of others. But often, those reflections are distorted by cultural conditioning and personal wounds. Over time, this can obscure the truth and goodness of who we really are.
- Often, we see goodness in others but don’t speak it aloud. And yet mirroring someone’s goodness can help them trust who they are.

#### Loving-kindness for others

- Even if it’s difficult to recognize this person’s goodness, remind yourself that all humans want to be happy, want to avoid suffering. Remember that life matters to this person, just as it does to you.
