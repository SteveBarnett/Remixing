## Introduction

- This is a book about getting unstuck.
- the system is designed, intentionally or not, to yield the results you got yesterday.
- Waste is defined as any activity that doesn’t add value for the customer.
- The curse of a bad set of habits is that all the unnecessary things you’re doing actually come to seem necessary.
- We’re stifled by the gravity of the way we’ve always done things (inertia). We consider so many potential possibilities for change that it freezes us (decision paralysis). We spend so much time fighting with colleagues about what we should do that we never actually accomplish anything (politics). And we exhaust ourselves chasing today’s problems, which always seem to crowd out tomorrow’s opportunities
(firefighting).
- We’ll call that “well-chosen something” a Leverage Point (a term popularized by the systems theorist Donella Meadows).
- The trick is to align all of those assets so that they push in the same direction. You need to Restack Resources on the Leverage Point.
- if you want to press harder on a Leverage Point, then you need to draw resources from something else you’re doing.
- To spark change, we shouldn’t think AND, we should think INSTEAD OF. Less of this, more of that.
- And those trade-offs are painful.
- As you apply this framework, you’ll likely encounter powerful obstacles: tradition and resistance and
bureaucracy and indifference
- **the progress principle: “Of all the things that can boost emotions, motivation, and perceptions during a workday, the single most important is making progress in meaningful work**.”
- Ultimately, the payoffs for our exploration ahead are simple but powerful: The relief of shaking off bad habits. The pleasure of experiencing movement where stasis had prevailed. The sudden snap of agency that comes from reminding yourself: I’m capable of changing this situation.
### Section 1 FIND LEVERAGE POINTS

### 1 Go and See the Work

> You can Find Leverage Points by **observing up close the reality of your work.**

- What we’re looking for are interventions that are both doable and worth doing.
- Glaring problems are sometimes the legacy of past solutions—improvisations and workarounds.
- what looks like “mismanagement” is often the accidental accretion of outdated habits.
- You’ll spot places where you and your team have acclimated to problems—instead of fixing them.
- For knowledge work, this type of observation can be harder.
- You will have to make it tangible by mapping out the flow:
- when unexpected problems arise in our organizations, it often reveals that we didn’t know as much about our “system” as we thought we did.
- The problem comes when we mistake functional understanding for systemic understanding. We think because we can use something, because it operates as we expect, that we understand it.

#### Whirlwind review: Chapter 1, Go and See the Work

- The first way to identify Leverage Points is to observe our work more closely. As Nelson Repenning says, “Go and see the work.”
- When you go and see the work, you often discover that you’ve been oblivious to problems, or have acclimated to them, instead of fixing them.
- Going and seeing the work is far preferable to the usual conference-room style of hatching improvements—what Tom Chi calls “guess-a-thons.”
- When you’re trying to improve your work, you’ll often discover that you have functional understanding rather than systemic understanding. Without that systemic understanding, it’s hard to make things better.
- But it’s possible to gain systemic understanding quickly. Turnaround consultants do it by going straight to the front lines.
- Ultimately, going and seeing the work is about getting closer to the reality of the situation and perceiving it with fresh eyes. That’s how you find promising Leverage Points.

### 2 Consider the Goal of the Goal

> You can Find Leverage Points by **identifying alternate pathways to your ultimate destination**.

- solutions-focused therapy. It’s called the “Miracle Question.”
- be warned: It usually requires some persistence. Usually, people hem and haw a bit and then leap to how they’d be feeling.

#### Whirlwind review: Chapter 2, Consider the Goal of the Goal

- The second way to identify Leverage Points is to take a careful and even skeptical look at the goals you’re pursuing.
- Often organizations set numerical targets as goals. Yet you can succeed at hitting these targets while utterly failing at your mission.
- To avoid that misalignment of goals and mission, you need to ask: What’s the goal of the goal? And are there alternate pathways that might get us there more easily or quickly than the original goal we’d selected?
- You can achieve more clarity about the “goal of the goal” by asking the Miracle Question.
- “What’s the goal of the goal?” helps you see the destination and why it’s important. (A Leverage Point must be something worth doing.) The Miracle Question helps you identify the first productive steps toward that destination. (A Leverage Point must be something doable.)
- Achieving clarity on where you’re really headed can be transformative.

### 3 Study the Bright Spots

> You can Find Leverage Points by **analyzing your own best work**.

- Averages are great for monitoring but terrible for diagnosis. Averages can tell us: Something’s wrong. But they’re unlikely to tell us what’s wrong, or how to fix it.
- Bright spots make great Leverage Points because you know they’re possible.

#### Whirlwind review: Chapter 3, Study the Bright Spots

- The third way to identify Leverage Points is to study the bright spots—your most successful efforts. If you analyze what allowed you to succeed in those occasions, you can scale that success.
- Averages are great for simple monitoring but terrible for diagnosis. If you want to spot Leverage Points, you must untangle averages.
- Often the way to unlock forward progress is to analyze your own best people or projects or performance.
- You can analyze bright spots on any level: bright spots in your own life, bright spots in a team, bright spots among different departments in one organization, or even bright spots across different organizations doing similar kinds of work.
- Finding Leverage Points can be a needle-in-a-haystack hunt. Some Leverage Points might seem promising but prove impossible. But bright spots are different—you know you can succeed, because you already have. (Can you do it more?)

### 4 Target the Constraint

> You can Find Leverage Points by **assessing the #1 force that is holding you back**.

- Where are there pileups or delays?
- Because constraints make sense only in the context of goals, a logical corollary is that when you shift your goal, the constraint shifts too.

#### Whirlwind review: Chapter 4, Target the Constraint

- The fourth way to identify Leverage Points is to target the “constraint,” or bottleneck—the #1 thing holding you back from your goal. Having identified the constraint, you can spot ideas for easing it.
- Constraints don’t go away. When you relieve one constraint, you improve the system AND another constraint takes its place. There’s always a constraint.
- Investments that aren’t directed at the constraint might do nothing to improve the system as a whole.
- Constraints can be clear-cut in operations-intensive environments. But every organization faces constraints, even if they aren’t as simple to quantify.
- Constraints only make sense in the context of goals. If you shift the goal, the constraint will move, too.
- When you understand deeply what is holding you back, you can spot possible Leverage Points that will allow you to shrink the obstacle and permit forward progress.

### 5 Map the System

> You can Find Leverage Points by **rising above the silos to spot hidden levers**.

- mapping the system works best when it spans silos, because no one (other than the top executives of an organization) is paying attention to the whole.
- One tried-and-true method for transcending silos is to follow the path of a key customer or constituent: Journey mapping

#### Whirlwind review: Chapter 5, Map the System

- The fifth and final way to identify Leverage Points is to map the system—zoom out to see the big picture. You’re hunting for the “hidden levers” in the system.
- Mapping the system requires ascending above the silos both within and across organizations.
- One reliable tool for mapping the system is to follow the journey of a customer (or patient or student) as they intersect different silos.
- The second critical aspect of mapping the system, in addition to rising above silos, is the need to challenge assumptions.
- By studying the way systems function—and the relationships between their parts—you can Find Leverage Points that unlock progress, even on a global scale.
### Section 2 RESTACK RESOURCES

### 6 Start with a Burst

> You can Restack Resources on your Leverage Points by **beginning with an intense and focused period of
work**.

- money, labor hours, physical assets, data, and so on. But those resources, deployed exactly as they are today, are not yielding the results you want. That’s why we have to restack them.
- A burst involves committing dedicated time to accomplish something meaningful.
- Task switching is one of those phenomena that have almost no redeeming features. Research shows that it makes us slower. Less effective. More prone to errors. More stressed.
- The themes of human behavior remain the same, even as the tools and details change.
- “teams who communicate in bursts—exchanging messages quickly during periods of high activity— perform much better than remote teams whose conversations involve long lag time between responses and are spread across multiple topic threads.”
- Miguel Brendl, a professor of marketing at the University of Basel. “Early in your pursuit of your goal, look backwards at what you have achieved; toward the end, look forward,”
- The ultimate effect of this “look backward, then look forward” strategy is to minimize the middle.
- shorten the middle.
- Setting team-level goals, rather than individual ones, united their efforts.

#### Whirlwind review: Chapter 6, Start with a Burst

- The first way to Restack Resources on Leverage Points is to start with an intense burst of effort. Think of dislodging a stuck window.
- Bursts are an antidote to the harms of “task switching”—continually moving back and forth between activities, which is inefficient and stressful.
- The strategic use of protected blocks of time can spark quick change.
- A burst involves working to completion. Your team accomplishes something tangible—even if it’s not “efficient.”
- Bursts elevate the team’s goals over the individual’s. That can spark more team spirit and better collaboration.
- The enemy of change efforts is the middle, when motivation lags. Bursts can help that by “shrinking the middle.”

### 7 Recycle Waste

> You can Restack Resources on your Leverage Points by **ending work that doesn’t serve the mission**.

- Waste is anything that doesn’t add value to your work in the customer’s eyes.
- eight possible categories of waste: Defects Overproduction Waiting Nonutilized talent Transportation Inventory Motion Excess processing
- This DOWNTIME model is grounded in the factory world, so it may not translate completely to your world.
- The particulars of the model are not as important as its central message: Wasteful activities can come in many colors.
- In nonfactory environments, probably the most critical component of the DOWNTIME framework is the N: Nonutilized talent. This category includes idle time. Distracted time. But its most profound meaning is this: It’s wasteful when people are playing below their level.

#### Whirlwind review: Chapter 7, Recycle Waste

- The second way to Restack Resources is to capture “waste,” which is any activity that doesn’t add value in the customer’s eyes.
- Waste comes in many varieties, and it’s worth obsessing about, because waste offers a free lunch: You can Restack Resources with no painful trade-off.
- In nonfactory environments, a critical area of waste is people playing below their level. You can correct that by “shifting right.”
- One note of caution: Shifting right can be taken too far. It can lead to soul-deadening overspecialization, as with the factory mindset of modern health care. But you can get it right on your team: People will tell you what’s good for them, if you ask them.
- Micromanagement is another near-universal kind of waste.
- How do you distinguish proper oversight from wasteful oversight? Jeff Bezos distinguished Type 1 decisions (big and nearly irreversible) from Type 2 decisions (readily reversible). It’s best not tooverthink Type 2 situations.

### 8 Do Less AND More

> You can Restack Resources on your Leverage Points by **shifting effort from lower-value work to higher-
value**.

- what we’re chasing, in particular, is a way to minimize the sting of those trade-offs.
- Here’s a haunting thought: What if the story is the same in our personal lives? What if we are undercoddling the most important people and overcoddling the least?
- You can fill in the quadrants with any kind of “thing”: ➞ STOP: The policy that it’s okay to bring pets to work. It’s getting smelly and weird. ➞ START: Using an outside law firm to handle contracts. It’s a constraint internally. ➞ MORE: Inside sales efforts in the Southeast. It’s working. ➞ LESS: Ginning up employee social events. Not many people seem to care.
- My friend said the hardest quadrants to address were LESS and, especially, STOP. Which is predictable. Usually, when we think about making changes, we’re thinking about adding something.
- in the early stages of change, you’ve got to be ambidextrous: Both cutting and investing. Both defense and offense. LESS and MORE.
- The worst strategy was a “cut only” approach. In these companies, “pessimism permeates the organization,”
- Almost as bad was a blithe “ignore the bad news and keep investing” strategy. With that kind of positive groupthink,
- The best strategy? Blending cuts and investments. LESS and MORE.

#### Whirlwind review: Chapter 8, Do Less AND More

- The third way to Restack Resources on Leverage Points is to shift them away from low-value activities. You need to simultaneously do MORE investing in your Leverage Points while investing LESS in the lower-priority activities.
- Reallocating your resources in a new direction can be painful because of the trade-offs involved. But if you look carefully, you can minimize the sting of those trade-offs.
- The consultancy Strategex has found that businesses consistently “overcoddle” their worst customers and “undercoddle” their best. This can be self-destructive given that the best 20% of customers might generate 150% of the profits.
- One simple exercise—in the spirit of generating smart trade-offs—is to ask your team to fill in four quadrants: STOP, START, LESS, MORE. Note that the “STOP” and “LESS” areas are the hardest ones.
- Restacking Resources is not just about “cutting” and it’s not just about “adding.” It’s about both: less AND more at the same time.

### 9 Tap Motivation

> You can Restack Resources on your Leverage Points by **prioritizing the work that’s required and desired**.

- attending to motivation is not a trick at all. It’s the very heart of change. To motivate people, we’ve got to pay attention to their desires. Their interests. Their hopes.
- Tapping motivation is about finding the intersection of “what’s required” and “what’s desired.”
- better to elicit action than simply to mandate it.
- As you make progress toward your new goal, make sure that what you celebrate is aligned with where you’re headed.
- Go where the energy is.
- visible progress changes minds.

#### Whirlwind review: Chapter 9, Tap Motivation

- The fourth way to Restack Resources on Leverage Points is to harness motivation. Think of it like a sun- drenched desert with no solar panels. It could provide valuable energy—but only if it’s tapped.
- If you want to fuel change, pay attention to what your constituents want, considering their interests and desires.
- Tapping motivation is about finding the intersection of “what’s required” and “what’s desired.” (Remember the Venn diagram illustrating that.)
- Your teammates will have different interests. How can you ensure that those interests can be channeled toward “what’s required”? Consider a “genius swap”: the idea that you can simply trade responsibilities according to interest.
- To Restack Resources on your Leverage Points, don’t think “How do I get buy-in for my idea?” Think, instead, “How could I harness the waterfall of available motivation to propel a new effort?”
- How do you handle change skeptics? Try: (1) picking a Leverage Point with more enthusiasts and fewer skeptics; (2) bargaining with the lukewarm or resistant factions; and/or (3) tapping the motivation of believers to make progress, and then count on those visible results to change minds.
- Progress is the spark that makes believers of skeptics.

### 10 Let People Drive

> You can Restack Resources on your Leverage Points by **giving your team the autonomy to own the
change effort**.

- This coaching is flipped. Instead of pushing ideas onto the athletes, Krueger pulls ideas from them. They set the goals, he doesn’t. They provide the assessment, he doesn’t.
- autonomy and alignment.
- two independent dimensions.

#### Whirlwind review: Chapter 10, Let People Drive

- Letting people drive is the fifth way to Restack Resources. Autonomy works because it’s motivating, it instills more accountability, it taps higher-order skills, and it reduces waste from micromanagement.
- In uncertain, anxiety-making situations, you may be tempted to grab for control. But that’s the wrong instinct: You need to let people drive.
- Autonomy is not an unbounded positive, though—either for the quality of the work or for the motivation of the employees. In a survey, people reported wanting guidance and support.
- Spotify has a nice way of blending freedom and guidance. They try to support a high-autonomy, high- alignment culture.
- It’s possible to shift to a high-autonomy, high-alignment culture with the right support systems and incentives.

### 11 Accelerate Learning

> You can Restack Resources on your Leverage Points by **getting better, faster feedback to fuel
improvements**.

- There’s an organic link between these threads of “faster learning” and “reduced waste.”
- People are lousy at estimating how long it will take to do something (the so-called “planning fallacy”).
- The book’s framework—Finding Leverage Points and Restacking Resources—is usually iterative. You identify a priority and then you push on it. If it doesn’t go the way you hoped, you learn from the experience. Maybe there’s a better direction to push.

#### Whirlwind review: Chapter 11, Accelerate Learning

- The sixth and final way to Restack Resources on your Leverage Points is to accelerate your learning. The faster you can learn, the faster you can go.
- The first question of accelerating learning is: How quickly can you identify failure?
- Useful feedback helps you align resources with goals. That tight calibration cuts waste, which is one of the key themes of Restacking.
- Learning faster doesn’t necessarily demand more data or technology. Sometimes it hinges on seeking out the people best equipped to give feedback.
- The second question of accelerating learning is: How quickly can you spot potential successes?
- The right information in the right moment is the ultimate navigational aid. If we want to make progress on what matters, we must accelerate our learning.
