Notes from R. B. Blakney's translation of the Tao Te Ching.

## Raw notes

### Preface

- "I cannot pretend to see the world as the Chinese of the third to sixth centuries before Christ saw it, and I do not believe any scientific sifting of the facts would enable a person of this century to do so. There will always remain a semantic gulf between them and us, one that must be bridged by adding insight and imagination to considered evidence."

### Introduction

- Confucius. It is no accident that China's great moral innovator was a gregarious man, who haunted capital cities, accompanied by disciples, pleading for a chance to set things right. Nor is it an accident that an important and original expression of the great mysticism of all time was conceived of in some of China's valleys.
- ... His meaning was that his life had been his prayer. If God was not moved by that, ritual prayers would add nothing. In its way, this was revolutionary.
- Reality, however designated, is One; it is an all-embracing unity from which nothing can be separated.
- In the universe, four things are great: the Way, the sky, the earth, the king. ... The king is alone among the four greats in needing admonition: he alone can depart from the Way.
- ... the basic traits of those who travel by the Way: humility, non-action, non-assertion, forgiveness, and benevolence to all.

### 1

Nature's secret, the constant, normative Way from which no event is exempt, is disclosed only to those who can be rid of personal wishes or prejudices about it. ... Prejudiced eyes are stopped cold at the surface.

> Brings trouble with it;<br>

### 24

The king will avoid temptations to stand "on tiptoe," i.e. to ambition, special advantages, exhibitionism, boasting, dictatorship, and pride.

### 25

The Way is really nameless; it is not a phenomenon, but the description ... suggests the lines of force in a magnetic field. They go out from one pole and return to the other.

### 63

The king achieves greatness by choosing hard things before they choose him.

### 67

> I have kept three treasures well secured:<br>
> The first, compassion; next, frugality;<br>
> And third, I say that never would I once<br>
> Presume that I should be the whole world's chief.

### 76

> Unbending rigour is the mate of death,<br>
> And yielding softness, company of life<br>
> ...<br>
> The strong and mighty topple from their place;<br>
> The soft and yielding rise above them all.
